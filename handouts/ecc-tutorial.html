01 / ORIENTATION

One operation, several protocols

ECC uses arithmetic on points of an elliptic curve. Starting with a public base point G, a secret integer d produces a public point Q = dG. Computing Q is efficient; recovering d from G and Q should be infeasible on a suitable real curve.

ECDH → agree on a secret

Two parties combine private scalars with public points to derive matching secret material.

ECDSA → authenticate a message

A private key signs a message digest; the public key checks the signature.

ECC is a mathematical foundation, not a complete encryption protocol. A secure system also needs hashes, a key derivation function (KDF), authenticated encryption, authentication, and careful implementation.

Prerequisites: remainders, basic algebra, binary numbers, and the purpose of public and private keys. By the end, you should be able to add curve points, compute a small scalar multiple, trace ECDH and ECDSA, and explain common implementation failures.

Teaching values only. The curve in this lesson has just 19 points. Its secrets can be recovered by inspection. The JavaScript calculator is readable teaching code, not constant-time cryptographic code.
02 / MATHEMATICAL BACKGROUND

Fields, characteristic, and curves

What is a field?

A field is a set with addition and multiplication in which you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide by any nonzero element, and the result stays in the set. These operations obey familiar algebra rules:

  • Addition and multiplication are associative and commutative.
  • There are identities 0 and 1, with 0 ≠ 1.
  • Every element a has an additive inverse −a, so a + (−a) = 0.
  • Every nonzero element a has a multiplicative inverse a⁻¹, so a · a⁻¹ = 1.
  • Multiplication distributes over addition: a(b + c) = ab + ac.

The rational numbers and real numbers are fields. The integers are not: 1 ÷ 2 is not an integer. A finite field has only finitely many elements, but still supports all these operations.

Our field: F₁₇

The finite field Fp, for prime p, contains the residues 0 through p − 1. Addition, subtraction, and multiplication reduce modulo p. For example, in F17, 15 + 5 = 3 and −3 = 14.

Division means multiplying by a modular inverse. A nonzero t has an inverse t−1 satisfying t · t−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Thus 2−1 = 9 modulo 17 because 2 · 9 = 18 ≡ 1. Zero has no inverse. The extended Euclidean algorithm finds inverses efficiently.

3 / 2 = 3 · 2⁻¹ = 3 · 9 = 27 ≡ 10 (mod 17)
Check: 2 · 10 = 20 ≡ 3 (mod 17).
The field supplies the coordinate arithmetic. The elliptic curve selects which coordinate pairs are valid points. These are separate mathematical objects.

What is the characteristic of a field?

The characteristic is the smallest positive number of times you must add 1 to itself to get 0. If no positive number of additions gives 0, the characteristic is defined to be 0.

  • Real and rational numbers: repeated addition of 1 never gives 0, so their characteristic is 0.
  • F₁₇: seventeen copies of 1 sum to 17 ≡ 0 (mod 17), and no smaller positive number does. Its characteristic is 17.

A field’s characteristic is always 0 or a prime. If the smallest positive count were composite, m = uv with 1 < u,v < m, then (u · 1)(v · 1) = m · 1 = 0 would multiply two nonzero elements to give zero. That cannot happen in a field: multiplying by the inverse of either nonzero factor would force the other factor to be zero.

Size and characteristic are different: a finite field with pm elements has characteristic p. For instance, a field with 2³ = 8 elements has characteristic 2; it is not the integers modulo 8, which do not form a field.

“Characteristic other than 2 or 3” means that 2 and 3 are nonzero field elements and therefore have inverses. This allows the usual reduction of a general elliptic-curve equation to the short Weierstrass form below. Our field F₁₇ satisfies this condition.

What is a singular curve?

Short Weierstrass curve (prime p > 3):
y² ≡ x³ + ax + b (mod p)
Require 4a³ + 27b² ≢ 0 (mod p).

A singular curve has a point where it is not smooth. Over the real numbers, familiar examples are a sharp cusp or a self-intersection. At a singular point, the usual construction using a unique tangent breaks down.

For y² = x³ + ax + b in characteristic other than 2 or 3, the curve is singular exactly when 4a³ + 27b² = 0 in the field. Over Fₚ, test the equality modulo p. For example, y² = x³ has a cusp at (0,0) over the real numbers: a = b = 0 makes the expression zero.

Our curve: a = b = 2, p = 17
4a³ + 27b² = 4·8 + 27·4 = 140 ≡ 4 (mod 17)
4 ≠ 0 → the curve is nonsingular.
Optional algebra: how do we detect a singular point without a picture?

Write F(x,y) = y² − x³ − ax − b. An affine point is singular when F = 0 and both partial derivatives vanish there: ∂F/∂x = −3x² − a = 0 and ∂F/∂y = 2y = 0. This definition works over finite fields too, where a smooth visual curve is unavailable. Eliminating x and y gives the condition 4a³ + 27b² = 0. Equivalently, the discriminant Δ = −16(4a³ + 27b²) vanishes. The point at infinity of this Weierstrass model is nonsingular.

Cryptographic elliptic curves must be nonsingular so the entire curve, including the point at infinity, supports the intended elliptic-curve group arithmetic.

From geometry to a group

Over the real numbers, drawing a line through two curve points, finding the third intersection, then reflecting vertically gives a geometric picture of addition. A tangent gives doubling. Over a finite field there is no continuous curve to trace: the same algebra operates on a finite set of coordinate pairs.

Add a special identity point O, called the point at infinity. The points form an abelian group: addition is associative and commutative, P + O = P, and each P has an inverse −P. For P = (x,y), −P = (x, −y mod p). O is not the coordinate pair (0,0).

03 / THE GROUP LAW

Add, double, and handle exceptions

Let P = (x₁,y₁), Q = (x₂,y₂), and R = P + Q = (x₃,y₃). All operations in this section are modulo p.

Addition, x₁ ≠ x₂:
λ = (y₂ − y₁)(x₂ − x₁)⁻¹

Doubling, P = Q and y₁ ≠ 0:
λ = (3x₁² + a)(2y₁)⁻¹

For either case:
x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂
y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁

Where do these formulas come from?

The x formula comes from substituting the line into the cubic and using the sum of its three roots. The y formula performs the reflection that defines the group sum. Here is the derivation, including what “roots” and “counting multiplicity” mean.

Step 1 · Turn intersections into an equation in x

For a nonvertical line through P and Q, write y = λx + c, where c = y₁ − λx₁. An intersection must satisfy both the line equation and the curve equation. Substitute the line into the curve:

(λx + c)² = x³ + ax + b

f(x) = x³ + ax + b − (λx + c)²
= x³ − λ²x² + (a − 2λc)x + (b − c²) = 0

A root of a polynomial is a value that makes it zero. If (x,y) is an intersection, its x-coordinate is a root of f. Conversely, if x is a root of f, then y = λx + c gives a point on both the line and the curve. Thus solving this cubic is exactly how we find intersection x-coordinates.

“Three intersections” does not necessarily mean three distinct points. A degree-three polynomial has three roots counting multiplicity over an algebraic closure (a field containing all its roots). In our addition construction, the two known roots—or the repeated root supplied by a tangent—are in the original field, so the remaining linear factor also gives a root in that field.

Step 2 · Understand roots and multiplicity

Since P and Q lie on the line and curve, x₁ and x₂ are roots. Write the third intersection as T = (xT,yT). Because f has leading coefficient 1, its factorization is:

f(x) = (x − x₁)(x − x₂)(x − x_T)

A root’s multiplicity is the number of times its factor appears. For example, (x − 2)²(x − 5) has roots 2, 2, 5 counting multiplicity, but only two distinct roots.

For doubling, the line is tangent at P: two intersections coincide there, so x₁ is a root at least twice. Usually the factorization is (x − x₁)²(x − xT), with a distinct third intersection. At an inflection point all three roots can coincide. This repeated-root interpretation is why the addition derivation also works when P = Q.

Step 3 · Read the sum of the roots from the coefficient

A polynomial is monic when its leading coefficient is 1. Expand the three factors:

(x − x₁)(x − x₂)(x − x_T)
= x³ − (x₁ + x₂ + x_T)x²
+ (x₁x₂ + x₁x_T + x₂x_T)x
− x₁x₂x_T

To obtain an x² term, choose x from two factors and the constant from the remaining factor. The three choices give −x₁x², −x₂x², and −xTx². Their sum explains the negative sign.

For x³ + Ax² + Bx + C:
sum of roots = −A

Here A = −λ², so:
x₁ + x₂ + x_T = λ²
x_T = λ² − x₁ − x₂

Step 4 · Reflect the third intersection

The group sum P + Q is defined as −T: keep the x-coordinate and negate the y-coordinate. Since T lies on the line:

y_T = λx_T + c = λ(x_T − x₁) + y₁

x₃ = x_T = λ² − x₁ − x₂
y₃ = −y_T = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁

Over the real numbers this is a reflection across the x-axis. Over Fₚ, “reflection” means the algebraic operation y → −y mod p.

See it on our toy curve

Take P = G = (5,1) and Q = 2G = (6,3). The line has slope 2 and equation y = 2x − 9. All calculations below are modulo 17.

f(x) = x³ + 2x + 2 − (2x − 9)²
= x³ − 4x² + 38x − 79
≡ (x − 5)(x − 6)(x − 10) (mod 17)

Root sum: 5 + 6 + 10 = 21 ≡ 4 = λ²
Third intersection: x_T = 10, y_T = 2·10 − 9 = 11
Reflect: −11 ≡ 6
P + Q = (10,6)
Intersection first, group sum second. The line’s third intersection is T = (10,11). The sum is its inverse −T = (10,6). Keeping these points distinct helps prevent sign errors.

Exceptional cases

  • Identity: P + O = O + P = P.
  • Opposites: P + (−P) = O; do not try to invert a zero denominator.
  • Vertical tangent: if y₁ = 0, then 2P = O.

For valid points, equal x coordinates mean either equal points or opposites, so these cases cover the possibilities. Production libraries often use projective coordinates to avoid repeated inversions; these affine formulas are easier to learn.

04 / A COMPLETE SMALL GROUP

y² = x³ + 2x + 2 over F₁₇

Choose G = (5,1). It lies on the curve: 1² = 1, and 5³ + 2·5 + 2 = 137 ≡ 1 (mod 17). The nonsingularity expression is 4·2³ + 27·2² = 140 ≡ 4 ≠ 0.

05 / EASY FORWARD, HARD BACKWARD

Scalar multiplication and the ECDLP

kG means adding G to itself k times, not multiplying its coordinates by k. Double-and-add uses the binary expansion of k and needs only O(log k) group operations.

13 = 1101₂ = 8 + 4 + 1
2G = (6,3), 4G = (3,1), 8G = (13,7)
8G + 4G = 12G = (0,11)
12G + G = 13G = (16,4)

A left-to-right version starts with R = O. For each bit from most significant to least: replace R with 2R; if the bit is 1, replace R with R + G. The lab traces this version.

The elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP) is: given G and Q = dG, find d. On this toy curve the table solves it immediately. For well-chosen large prime-order groups, generic classical attacks such as Pollard rho take about √n operations. An order near 2²⁵⁶ therefore targets roughly 128-bit classical security, not 256-bit security.

Large size alone is insufficient: the curve, subgroup, protocol, and implementation all matter. A sufficiently capable quantum computer running Shor’s algorithm would break both ECC and RSA.

07 / KEY AGREEMENT

ECDH: meet at the same point

Alice keeps a private scalar α and sends A = αG. Bob keeps β and sends B = βG. Alice computes αB; Bob computes βA. Both equal αβG by associativity of scalar multiplication.

A complete toy exchange
StepAliceBob
Private scalarα = 5β = 7
Public pointA = 5G = (9,16)B = 7G = (0,6)
Shared point5B = 35G = 16G7A = 35G = 16G
Result(10,11)(10,11)

The arithmetic wraps modulo n for the scalar: 35 ≡ 16 (mod 19). In a real protocol, the specified encoding of the shared result feeds a KDF, together with protocol context such as identities and transcript information. Do not use a raw coordinate directly as an encryption key.

Agreement is not authentication. An active attacker can replace both public keys and establish separate secrets with each party. Authenticate the exchange using a proven protocol with certificates, signatures, or a preshared key. Fresh ephemeral keys can provide forward secrecy when their secrets are erased and the protocol is designed correctly.
08 / DIGITAL SIGNATURES

ECDSA, with every number exposed

Use a base point G of prime order n, private key d ∈ {1,…,n−1}, and public key Q = dG. Hash the message and convert the digest to integer z as specified by the scheme: when the digest is longer than the bit length of n, keep its leftmost bits of that length. Subsequent signature arithmetic is modulo n. Our example stipulates z = 13; it does not pretend that a tiny toy digest is secure.

Sign

  1. Generate a secret nonce k ∈ {1,…,n−1} using a secure specified method.
  2. Compute R = kG and r = xR mod n. Retry with a new valid nonce if r = 0.
  3. Compute s = k−1(z + rd) mod n. Retry if s = 0. Output (r,s).
n = 19, d = 7, Q = 7G = (0,6), z = 13, k = 3
R = 3G = (10,6) → r = 10
3⁻¹ mod 19 = 13
s = 13(13 + 10·7) mod 19
= 13·83 mod 19 = 15
Signature: (r,s) = (10,15)

Verify

  1. Validate Q and require integers 1 ≤ r,s ≤ n−1. Recompute z from the message.
  2. Compute w = s−1 mod n, u₁ = zw mod n, and u₂ = rw mod n.
  3. Compute X = u₁G + u₂Q. Reject if X = O.
  4. Accept exactly when xX mod n = r.
w = 15⁻¹ mod 19 = 14 (15·14 = 210 ≡ 1)
u₁ = 13·14 mod 19 = 11
u₂ = 10·14 mod 19 = 7
X = 11G + 7Q = (11 + 7·7)G = 60G = 3G
= (10,6)
x_X mod 19 = 10 = r → accept

One coordinate check: 11G = (13,10), 7Q = 49G = 11G, so X is the double of (13,10). Its slope is 509 · 20−1 ≡ 16 · 6 ≡ 11 (mod 17), which gives (10,6).

Why it works: u₁G + u₂Q = s−1(z + rd)G = kG. The verifier reconstructs R without knowing d or k. If the message representative changes to z = 14, the same signature instead yields X = 17G = (6,14), so verification fails because 6 ≠ 10.

Nonce reuse exposes the private key

If the same k signs two different digest representatives under the same key, both signatures have the same r. Provided the denominators below are nonzero modulo n:

k = (z₁ − z₂)(s₁ − s₂)⁻¹ mod n
d = (s₁k − z₁)r⁻¹ mod n

Reuse our k = 3 with z₂ = 14: s₂ = 13(14 + 70) mod 19 = 9. An observer of (10,15) and (10,9) recovers k = (13−14)·(15−9)−1 = −1·16 ≡ 3, then d = (15·3−13)·10−1 = 32·2 ≡ 7.

Biased, predictable, or partly leaked nonces can also reveal keys. Use a vetted implementation of an approved random-nonce method or a deterministic construction such as RFC 6979. Deterministic does not mean public: the nonce depends on the private key and message digest. It does not remove side-channel risks or the need for secure key generation.

(secp256r1)		A widely interoperable NIST curve for ECDH and ECDSA, targeting about 128-bit classical security. Select it when the protocol or compliance environment calls for it.

X25519 and Ed25519 are different algorithms with different encodings and purposes. Do not interchange their keys. For either real-world choice, use maintained cryptographic libraries and established protocols. See RFC 7748 and NIST SP 800-186.

Validate keys according to the algorithm

  • Conventional full-point ECDH/ECDSA: parse the specified encoding strictly; reject O as a public key; check canonical coordinate ranges, the curve equation, and appropriate subgroup membership (nQ = O). Fix approved domain parameters instead of accepting attacker-selected ones. For P-256’s cofactor-one group, a valid nonidentity on-curve point already has the required order.
  • X25519: follow RFC 7748’s input decoding and scalar processing, rather than grafting on Weierstrass point checks. Use a protocol/library that checks and rejects an all-zero shared output where required (TLS 1.3 requires this). Do not incorrectly reject every noncanonical input: X25519 specifies how such inputs are processed.
  • Key identity: a mathematically valid public key does not prove whose key it is. Validate certificates or other authenticated bindings, and separate keys by purpose.

Protect secrets beyond the equations

Secret-dependent branches, table indices, or memory access can leak through timing, caches, power, or electromagnetic measurements. The lab’s branch on each scalar bit intentionally illustrates an unsafe implementation pattern. Production code needs constant-time arithmetic and suitable hardened algorithms; merely using a fixed number of loop iterations is not enough. Fault defenses and scalar blinding may be relevant to the threat model.

Generate private keys with a cryptographically secure source, protect and erase secret material when feasible, validate inputs before sensitive operations, and use authenticated encryption for data. Never implement production ECC by enlarging this demo’s numbers: JavaScript Number also loses integer precision above 2⁵³−1.

Neither X25519 nor P-256 is post-quantum secure. Long-lived systems should follow their ecosystem’s standardized post-quantum migration guidance, including protocol-defined hybrid key agreement where appropriate.

11 / RETRIEVAL PRACTICE

Check your understanding

Work each question before opening its answer. Suggested class sequence: 15 minutes of field arithmetic, 20 minutes of group exercises and the lab, 20 minutes of protocols, then a security discussion.

1. What is 5⁻¹ modulo 17? What is 3 / 5?

5⁻¹ = 7 because 5·7 = 35 ≡ 1. Therefore 3 / 5 = 3·7 = 21 ≡ 4 (mod 17).

2. Find −G, G + (−G), and 20G.

−G = (5,16); G + (−G) = O; 20G = (19+1)G = G = (5,1).

3. Compute 4G by doubling 2G = (6,3).

λ = (3·36+2)·6⁻¹ = 110·3 ≡ 7. Then x = 49−12 ≡ 3 and y = 7(6−3)−3 ≡ 1. Hence 4G = (3,1).

4. Why do coordinates use modulo 17 but ECDSA scalars use modulo 19?

17 is the field size. 19 is the order of G and controls scalar equivalence. They describe different algebraic objects and are not interchangeable.

5. ECDH with private scalars 2 and 9: what is the shared point?

Both parties obtain 18G = (5,16). Their public points are 2G = (6,3) and 9G = (7,6). Authentication is still needed.

6. Does a 256-bit ECC key give 256-bit classical security?

No. A well-chosen subgroup of order about 2²⁵⁶ usually targets about 128 bits because generic discrete-log attacks take about √n work.

7. Why is a fresh but predictable ECDSA nonce unsafe?

Knowing k gives d = (sk−z)r⁻¹ mod n from one signature. Nonces must be secret and generated by a secure specified method; uniqueness alone is insufficient.

8. A peer supplies an on-curve public point. Is it trustworthy?

Not necessarily. The algorithm may also require nonidentity and subgroup checks, and the protocol must authenticate ownership. Validation and authentication solve different problems.

9. Why can’t this lab be reused with real private keys?

It uses tiny parameters, variable-time arithmetic and branches, and JavaScript Number. It has no production key handling, secure randomness, protocol authentication, or implementation hardening.

10. Extension: explain why the ECDSA verification point equals kG.

Substitute Q = dG into u₁G + u₂Q to get s⁻¹(z+rd)G. The signing equation gives z+rd ≡ sk (mod n), so the result is kG.

11. Is the set of integers a field? What is the characteristic of F₁₇?

The integers are not a field because nonzero integers do not always have multiplicative inverses within the integers; for example, 2⁻¹ = 1/2 is not an integer. F₁₇ is a field of characteristic 17.

12. Is y² = x³ singular? Is our curve over F₁₇ singular?

For y² = x³, a = b = 0, so 4a³ + 27b² = 0: it is singular at (0,0). For our curve, the expression is 140 ≡ 4 modulo 17, so it is nonsingular.

13. What does “counting multiplicity” mean for (x − 2)²(x − 5)?

The roots are 2, 2, and 5; the root 2 has multiplicity two. Their sum is 9, and expanding gives x³ − 9x² + 24x − 20, whose x² coefficient is −9.

14. Why isn’t the third intersection T itself the group sum?

The elliptic-curve addition rule defines P + Q = −T. Negating T preserves its x-coordinate and negates its y-coordinate, giving y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁.
